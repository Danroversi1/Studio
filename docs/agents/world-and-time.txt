## 2. Purpose of the section

World & Time establishes the narrative’s overall setting:

**Where does this story happen, when does it happen, and how much time does it span?**

Example:
“The story follows a character in Los Angeles in 2046 and again in 2056, spanning ten years of their life.”

The primary experience defines the world at this level. Detailed scene geography, travel times, and event chronology belong in an optional deeper subsection.

Keep these concepts separate:

| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Film runtime | How long the audience watches |
| Geographical setting | Where the narrative happens |
| Era or periods | When the narrative happens |
| Narrative span | Time between the earliest and latest story events |
| Temporal coverage | Whether we follow that span continuously or visit selected moments |
| Presentation order | The order in which the audience discovers events |

A five-minute film can span ten years. Two periods ten years apart do not imply that every intervening year appears.

## 4. Main overview

Make three editable summary cards the core of the page:

### WHERE

Capture the narrative’s geographical setting.

Support:
- One or multiple places.
- Real, invented, and mixed settings.
- Different scales, from a room to several planets.
- Nested places when useful.
- Unknown or undecided settings.

Example:
**Los Angeles**
“One city, two moments.”

Reuse existing location IDs where appropriate. Distinguish fictional settings from actual filming locations.

### WHEN

Capture one or more narrative periods.

Support:
- Calendar years or dates.
- Approximate eras.
- Relative descriptions.
- Invented calendars.
- Multiple distinct periods.
- “Not decided.”

Example:
**2046 & 2056**
“Two story periods.”

Retain the filmmaker’s original description alongside any normalized values.

### TIME SPAN

Show the elapsed narrative duration.

Examples:
- One afternoon.
- Ten years.
- Several generations.
- Not decided.

Calculate a suggested span only when the available information supports it. Label calculations appropriately; do not imply exact precision from approximate dates.

Allow correction. If a manually entered duration conflicts with dates, flag it for clarification rather than silently overwriting either value.

Do not force unusual time systems into one misleading number. Different characters or worlds may experience different elapsed durations.

### Narrative summary

Below the cards, show:

**YOUR WORLD, IN A SENTENCE**

Generate a concise, editable synthesis from confirmed information.

Example:
“A character’s life in Los Angeles, seen in 2046 and again in 2056.”

Do not invent missing facts. Preserve the director’s edits. Mark unaccepted interpretations as suggestions.

### Optional visual reference

Allow a reference image, drawing, or mood board through the platform’s existing upload/reference system.

Keep this optional and secondary to the setting definition.

Label generated or speculative imagery clearly. A future-city image is an imagined reference, not a factual prediction.

Do not claim to inspect images available only as inaccessible links.

## 5. Empty and partially populated states

Read the project’s available synopsis, treatment, script, and confirmed settings before asking questions.

If the setting is explicitly established by the filmmaker, reuse it.

If it is inferred or uncertain, show it as a suggestion to review. Do not convert an AI inference into a confirmed fact.

For a new project, begin with a simple invitation:
“Where do you imagine your story happening?”

Show only relevant unresolved areas. Avoid a dashboard of empty mandatory fields, completeness scores, or error states for creative uncertainty.

Provide direct editing as well as conversational development. Users must be able to define their setting without using an agent.

## 6. World Builder conversation

Integrate with the existing agent system and model provider. Reuse conversation, streaming, and error-handling components where available.

The agent helps a 13-year-old develop her own ideas. It should:
- Speak clearly and respectfully.
- Ask one focused question at a time.
- Build on her latest answer.
- Reuse information already provided.
- Offer possibilities without deciding for her.
- Accept “I don’t know.”
- Suggest a small drawing or writing experiment when useful.
- Keep ordinary responses brief.
- Preserve her authorship.

Use this conversational pattern:

**Listen → reflect → explore a consequence or alternative → let her choose → update.**

Draw from questions such as:
- What makes this place important to your character?
- Why does the story happen in that period?
- Do we follow those years as they happen, or visit a few important moments?
- Does the place change, or does your character experience it differently?
- What stays familiar across both periods?
- What does this setting make possible—or difficult?

Treat these as a private question bank, not a checklist.

Do not require a changing city, elaborate history, futuristic technology, or world rules merely because those topics are available.

If real-world research is needed, use available research tools and distinguish verified information from speculation. Without access, identify uncertainty.

## 7. Runtime-aware development

Read the film runtime established elsewhere in the project.

If it is unknown, ask briefly before extensive world development and save the answer through the shared project mechanism. Do not create a second conflicting runtime field.

For short films:
- Concentrate on the few setting details the audience needs.
- Help communicate a large span economically.
- Avoid unnecessary worldbuilding.
- Offer small exercises tied to the story.

Examples:
- Show ten years passing through one object.
- Draw the same place in two periods.
- Choose one thing that changes and one that remains.

Keep expansion ideas in the existing “For later” area, or an equivalent lightweight space.

Do not discourage ambitious ideas simply because the filmmaker is young. Help her find an achievable expression of them.

## 8. Example interaction

Existing confirmed information:
Los Angeles; 2046 and 2056; ten-year span.

Agent:
“What makes Los Angeles in 2056 feel different from 2046?”

Optional response suggestions:
- The city has changed.
- My character sees it differently.
- A bit of both.

Always allow free text and “Not sure yet.”

If she selects a changed perspective, explore her character’s relationship to the city. Do not automatically invent architectural, political, or technological changes.

An explicit decision can be recorded immediately. Do not ask for redundant confirmation after every answer. Agent suggestions remain provisional until adopted.

## 11. Connections to other modules

- Characters.
- Production Design.

Do not silently age characters, rewrite scenes, alter approved designs, or invent events between periods.

- What may need review.
